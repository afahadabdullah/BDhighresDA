\section{Adding error to the pseudo-observations}
\label{sec:osse-error}

\subsection{What ``adding error'' does, per stream}

The OSSE draws both observation types from the CHIRPS nature run and then, under
\texttt{--obs-error realistic}, perturbs them. The two streams are perturbed by
different mechanisms, and the difference turns out to matter more than anything
else in this section.

\paragraph{Gauges: white noise, drawn once per station-day.}
\begin{verbatim}
gauge_obs_transformed = gauge_truth_transformed
                      + rng.normal(0.0, gauge_noise_sd, n_stations)
gauge_noise_sd = sqrt(sigma_obs**2 + representativeness**2)
\end{verbatim}
With the configured $\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}} = 0.10$ and representativeness
$0.25$, that is $\sqrt{0.10^2 + 0.25^2} = 0.269$ in transformed units, spatially
independent.

\paragraph{Satellite: correlated noise, drawn from the INFLATED $R$.}
\begin{verbatim}
R[len(active_assim_idx):] *= satellite_r_inflation    # 6.28x at stride 3
...
satellite_observed = perturb_observations(
    satellite_truth_transformed, satellite_R, 1, corr_blocks=[...])
\end{verbatim}
The base error is $\sqrt{0.35^2 + 0.10^2} = 0.364$. But the same $R$ that was
inflated by $\max(1, 2\pi(\ell/s)^2)$ --- in order to \emph{down-weight}
correlated observations in the analysis --- is then used to \emph{generate} the
observation.

Under the \texttt{log1p} transform an additive shift in transformed space is a
\emph{multiplicative} error on rainfall. Taking $\mathrm{sd} = 1$ for
illustration, one standard deviation of injected error multiplies
$(p + \varepsilon)$ by $1.31$ for the gauges, $2.49$ for the satellite at
stride 3, and $15.45$ at stride 1. A $20$~mm\,day$^{-1}$ footprint becomes
$50$~mm\,day$^{-1}$ at stride 3 and $310$~mm\,day$^{-1}$ at stride 1. The
``pseudo-IMERG product'' is not a noisy satellite retrieval; at stride 1 it is
not rainfall.

\paragraph{Gauges tolerate their error; the satellite does not.}
This is the whole result. A $0.269$ white error costs the gauge arm nothing it
cannot absorb --- it still improves CRPS by $14\%$ at withheld stations, the
full field by $26\%$, and correlation by $+0.11$. A $0.912$ correlated error
takes the satellite arm to $-68\%$ CRPS and $-146\%$ RMSE. Adding the gauges
back on top recovers the gridded scores almost to neutral ($-2.1\%$ field) but
not the point scores ($-38.7\%$): the gauges repair the field where they are,
and cannot repair it where they are not.

\paragraph{An uninformative observation cannot do this.}
Assimilating an observation carrying no usable information should drive the
analysis toward the prior and converge on zero improvement. It cannot push the
analysis $68\%$ \emph{below} the prior. Degradation of that size requires the
likelihood to be pulling the state confidently toward somewhere the truth is
not, which is what an error drawn from an inflated $R$ and then assimilated
under that same inflated $R$ produces: the analysis is told the observation is
imprecise, but the observation is \emph{also} wrong by exactly that much, and
the two do not cancel.

\subsection{The cause, established from the code}

An earlier draft proposed that the pseudo-observations were built by averaging
\emph{transformed} CHIRPS while the operator averages in physical space, making
$T(\overline{x})$ and $\overline{T(x)}$ inconsistent. \textbf{That hypothesis is
wrong} and is recorded here only so it is not proposed again:
\texttt{scripts/10\_osse.py} computes \texttt{block\_mean\_mm} followed by
\texttt{transform.forward}, which is exactly what
\texttt{PhysicalBlockAverageObsOperator} predicts. The two are consistent.

The cause is the double use of the correlation inflation shown above. The
inflation exists to down-weight observations whose errors are not independent;
using it to \emph{build} the observation means that asking for a more
conservative $R$ makes the synthetic data worse, and the inflation is then
counted twice --- once in the values, once in the weight. An OSSE should
generate the observation with its \emph{true} error and assimilate it under
whatever $R$ is being tested.

\paragraph{This accounts for the one anomaly.}
Subgrid correlation \emph{improves} with pseudo-IMERG ($+0.038$, against
$+0.018$ for gauges alone) even as everything else collapses. Subtracting each
field's own $0.1^\circ$ footprint mean removes most of a footprint-scale
correlated error, so the residual is comparatively clean. A magnitude error
alone would not produce that; a \emph{footprint-scale correlated} one does.

\paragraph{The same mechanism, now seen twice.}
This is the $\sqrt{R}$ pathology already established on real data, where stride
1 at $0.1^\circ$ degraded CRPS by $+2.388~[+1.824, +2.956]$ because
\texttt{perturb\_observations} draws with $\mathrm{sd} = \sqrt{R}$ and a
$56.5\times$ inflation multiplied the perturbation amplitude by $7.5$. It has
now appeared in two independent code paths --- once perturbing ensemble members
in the real-data assimilation, once constructing a synthetic observation in the
OSSE --- which makes it a property of how $R$ is used throughout this system
rather than a local slip.

\subsection{What this means for reading any OSSE result with error}

Three consequences worth stating explicitly, because they apply beyond this
experiment.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Error settings that pass through $R$ are not a clean strength
    axis.} Any knob that scales $R$ also scales the error injected into the
    synthetic observation, so a ``down-weighting'' experiment is silently also
    a ``corrupt the data'' experiment. The two effects have opposite intent and
    the same control.
  \item \textbf{Thinning and error magnitude are entangled.} The inflation is
    $\propto (\ell/s)^2$, so halving the stride quadruples the injected error.
    A stride sweep is therefore not a sweep over observation \emph{density} but
    over density and corruption together --- which is exactly the confound that
    made the real-data \texttt{S1} arm uninterpretable until the mechanism was
    identified.
  \item \textbf{Correlated error is far more damaging than white error of the
    same nominal size.} The gauge stream absorbs $0.269$; the satellite stream
    is destroyed by $0.912$. Some of that is the $3.4\times$ magnitude, but the
    structure matters independently: white error across many observations
    averages down in the analysis, correlated error does not.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Predictions for the perfect-observation arms}
\label{sec:osse-error-predictions}

Arms 0--2 of \texttt{slurm/osse\_exact\_bmd.sbatch} repeat this experiment with
\texttt{--obs-error exact}, which perturbs neither stream and asserts that the
pseudo-satellite equals the same-day CHIRPS block mean. They are not yet run.
Recording the expectations before the fact, so the diagnosis above is
falsifiable:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \texttt{gauges\_exact\_bmd} should improve on
    \texttt{gauges\_realistic\_bmd}'s $+14\%$, since the only change is the
    removal of a $0.269$ error. A large improvement would also quantify how
    much of the real-data gauge arm's ceiling is observation error rather than
    prior quality.
  \item \texttt{satellite\_exact\_bmd} should be \emph{strongly positive}. It
    assimilates the truth's own $0.1^\circ$ block means at every footprint. If
    it is, the defect is confirmed and localised to observation generation, and
    the operator, the geometry and the likelihood are all exonerated. \textbf{If
    it is still negative, the diagnosis in this section is wrong} and the fault
    lies in the operator or the likelihood.
  \item \texttt{simultaneous\_exact\_bmd} should be no worse than the better of
    the two single-source arms. Perfect information from two consistent sources
    cannot legitimately be worse than either alone; if it is, the composite
    likelihood is mis-weighting them, which is a separate defect from the one
    described here and would not have been visible while the satellite error
    dominated everything.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Consequence for the paper}

No arm containing perturbed pseudo-IMERG can currently be reported: the
simultaneous arm is also below its background at withheld gauges
($-38.65\%$). The OSSE has therefore been restructured to run perfect
observations first, with error arms retained as sensitivities to be interpreted
only once the perfect arms establish what correct information buys.

This does \emph{not} affect the real-data results in
Section~\ref{sec:ingestion}, which use actual IMERG through
\texttt{scripts/15\_bmd\_month\_example.py} and never touch the OSSE's
pseudo-observation generator. It does mean the OSSE cannot yet serve its
intended role in the argument --- explaining the real-data null by locating the
observation-error threshold at which a satellite starts to help --- because the
error axis it would sweep is the same axis that corrupts the data. Fixing the
generation path is a prerequisite for that experiment, not an optional tidy-up.
