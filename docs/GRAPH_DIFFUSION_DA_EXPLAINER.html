Swap the convolutional backbone for message passing on a mesh, and data assimilation stops being a gridded problem. Observations become nodes. The observation operator becomes an encoder. And localisation stops being a tunable radius — it becomes the network's receptive field.

01

Why leave the grid

A convolutional prior on a regular latitude–longitude grid is a good fit for a small rectangular domain — that is exactly what a U-Net over a 128×128 patch is for. Take the same idea global and three things break at once, and all three are fixed by the same move.

The same sphere, two discretisationsdrag either globe to rotate
Regular 10° lat–lon grid — cells collapse at the poles
3-refined icosahedral mesh — near-uniform cells everywhere
Geometry

A convolution is not the same size twice

On a lat–lon grid a 3×3 kernel spans ~300 km at the equator and ~15 km at 85°N. The network spends capacity learning that the same operator means different physics at different latitudes, and the poles are oversampled to the point of instability.

mesh cells within ~10% of each other
Range

Long-range coupling in few hops

A convolutional stack reaches far only by stacking layers or downsampling. A multi-mesh carries the edges of every refinement level at once, so a single message-passing round moves information both one cell and a quarter of the globe.

GraphCast: meshes M0 … M6 superimposed
Observations

Data that never sat on a grid

A radiosonde profile, a satellite swath, a buoy — none of them land on cell centres. On a grid you interpolate and lose the error structure. On a graph the observation is a node, with its own features, its own error, and edges to the mesh.

GraphDOP: obs nodes as the primary input

The companion to this page works the other way round: SURMA-Flow — a rectified-flow U-Net on a fixed 128×128 grid over one country, guided by rain gauges and satellite footprints. Same posterior, same score-guidance algebra, different state representation. Section 07 below puts the two designs side by side and says which one to build when.

02

The mesh, and how far a message travels

This is the most consequential thing to understand about graph-based DA, and it is invisible in the equations. Click any node. The highlight shows every node reachable in K hops — and because the guidance gradient is backpropagated through exactly K message-passing rounds, that highlighted set is the entire region a single observation can influence. Localisation is not a covariance radius you tune. It is the architecture.

Real systems are far denser than this: GraphCast's finest mesh has 40,962 nodes; GenCast runs a 5-refined mesh of 10,242 nodes and 61,440 edges, with 16 processor blocks. Its attention is deliberately restricted to a 16-hop mesh neighbourhood — a localisation choice written into the architecture.

03

Encoder, processor, decoder

The backbone has three parts and only the middle one is deep. The encoder maps whatever you have — grid cells, observation reports, or both — onto the mesh through bipartite edges. The processor does all the work on the mesh, where the geometry is uniform. The decoder reads the mesh back out wherever you want an answer, which need not be where the inputs were.

Three details that are easy to skip and expensive to get wrong

Edges carry geometry. Relative displacement in a local frame at the receiving node, not absolute coordinates — that is what makes the same learned operator valid at the equator and over Antarctica.

The noise level conditions everything. GenCast replaces every layer-norm with a conditional layer-norm driven by an embedding of log σ. A denoiser that does not know how noisy its input is cannot possibly return the right correction.

Sample the noise on the sphere, not on the grid. Drawing i.i.d. noise per lat–lon cell puts enormous power at the poles. Sampling isotropically on the sphere gives a flat spherical-harmonic spectrum, which is what "white noise" is supposed to mean on a globe.

04

The prior: a denoiser, read as a score

Training is the ordinary denoising objective. Corrupt a real field with noise of a randomly drawn level σ, and ask the network to recover it. Nothing about assimilation enters here. What makes the trained denoiser useful afterwards is one identity: a denoiser is a score function in disguise.

Why the schedule is so front-loaded

Most of the 20 steps are spent at small σ. Above σ ≈ 10 the state is essentially noise and the denoiser is just sketching the largest scales; the schedule races through that and then slows down where the field's detail is actually decided.

This is also why observations barely matter early. Their weight is set by how much of the state is signal, and at σ = 80 almost none of it is. Section 05 plots that directly.

05

Assimilation: add a second score

Bayes' rule in score form is one line, and it is the whole method. The gradient of the log-posterior is the gradient of the log-prior plus the gradient of the log-likelihood. The trained denoiser supplies the first term for free. The second term is where the observations live — and evaluating it means differentiating the observation mismatch back through every message-passing block.

Route A — guidance

Keep the prior frozen and differentiate the Gaussian mismatch through it (diffusion posterior sampling). Nothing is retrained, any observation operator works, and a new instrument is a new node type rather than a new model.

The costs are real: two to three times the evaluations per step, a backward pass through all K blocks held in memory, and a likelihood that is only approximately right at intermediate σ. γ and the guidance scale are tuning knobs that quietly change the method.

Route B — replay

Where observations are direct measurements of state components, the observed entries can be overwritten at each denoising step with a correctly-noised draw of the observation, and the prior fills in the rest. No gradient, no γ.

It needs H to be a projection, which real satellite radiances are not, and it tends to leave seams between observed and unobserved regions. Most practical systems mix the two.

Route C — measurement-aware

A newer line of work changes the forward process so it interpolates between state space and measurement space, putting the observations into the training objective rather than bolting them on at sampling time. The likelihood score is then analytic for linear H.

It removes the DPS approximation and the guidance-scale tuning, at the price of a prior that is no longer observation-agnostic — retrain when the observing system changes.

Where the graph earns its keep: in route A the gradient can only travel as far as the network's receptive field. Turn K down to 1 above and the increments become isolated dots at the observations; turn it up and they spread into coherent structures. On a grid you would impose that with a localisation radius and a covariance model. Here you get it — for better and for worse — from the mesh connectivity and the block count.

06

Cycling, and what has to be checked

One analysis is not data assimilation. The point is the cycle: the analysis ensemble becomes the initial condition, the model propagates it, spread grows, the next batch of observations pulls it back. A diffusion model on a graph can do both halves with the same backbone — the forecast step is the same denoiser conditioned on the previous state instead of on observations.

Six cycles · schematicerror and spread, normalised units

Making it four-dimensional

The clean version is not to filter at all. Give the graph a time axis — nodes at several valid times, edges between consecutive ones — and learn a score over the whole trajectory. Observations anywhere in the window then guide a single sample of the entire window, which is the generative analogue of 4D-Var, without a tangent-linear or adjoint model.

The price is the obvious one: the state you are denoising is now the window, not the step, and both memory and the backward pass scale with it.

The circularity to declare

Almost every diffusion prior in this space is trained on reanalysis — itself the output of a conventional assimilation system. So the prior has already absorbed the observation types, the bias corrections and the error assumptions of that system.

That is not disqualifying, but it means agreement with reanalysis is not verification, in exactly the way that agreement with a training target never is. It is also the main argument for models trained directly on observations.

07

So which one should you build?

The companion page describes a different design point for the same posterior: SURMA-Flow — a conditional rectified-flow U-Net on a fixed regional grid, guided by gauges and satellite footprints. It is not a smaller version of a graph diffusion model, and it is not strictly worse. On some axes it is clearly better, and the reasons are specific enough to check.

The one that is not a matter of taste — how much of the trajectory the observations can act on
Rectified flow · steps where obs carry >½ weight—of 50 Heun steps
EDM diffusion · steps where obs carry >½ weight—of 20 denoising steps

With the linear interpolant, the likelihood variance is R + Γ(1−t)²/t², which reaches R exactly at t = 1 and is already small by the middle of the path. With EDM noise levels running from σ = 80, R + γσ² stays enormous until very late: most of the denoising budget is spent where the observations cannot bite, so the increment has to be delivered in a handful of steps.

Be precise about what this shows: it is a property of the schedule and noise parameterisation, not of flow-versus-diffusion in the abstract. You can re-tune γ, or compress the σ schedule, and move the blue curve left. The point is that the bounded interpolant gives you this for free, while σ_max = 80 was chosen for generative sample quality, not for assimilation.

The one-sentence answer. For a bounded domain with a handful of well-understood observation types, where the analysis has to be defended station by station and the compute is one node, the rectified-flow design wins on every axis that matters — exact operators, a prior anchored to a coarse analysis, a localisation you can quote, and observations that act over most of the trajectory. Scale the domain to the sphere, or the observing system to everything that reports, and every one of those advantages turns into a limitation.
